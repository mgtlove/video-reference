## What it is, in two sentences

Dev Snap's CSS Animation Examples page is a curated wall of 74 public CodePen pens, each embedded with its title and author, spanning line drawing, text effects, 3D transforms, card reveals, icon micro-animations and illustrated scenes, mostly from 2014 to 2020. It is a browsing aid, not a library: the site writes nothing of its own, so the value is in the pens and their authors, and the licence question goes to CodePen rather than to Dev Snap.

## Techniques this source points to

For each technique, one paragraph: what it is, how it is done in plain terms, a first opinion on the rig questions (seekable, deterministic, offline, tokens, cost, plain files) marked as **unmeasured** unless a study exists, and the study it links to in `studies/` if there is one.

**Line drawing with a normalised path length.** The keyframers pen is the clearest statement of the trick: give every SVG path a path length of 1, dash it with a dash length of 1, and animate the dash offset from 1 to 0. The line draws itself and the same keyframes work for paths of any real length, which makes a diagram with twenty strokes a matter of delays rather than measurements. Pure CSS on SVG, so pausing at a time offset and re-rendering should be exact. Rig questions: **unmeasured**. No study yet.

**Handwriting and signature strokes.** Same dash offset idea with one stroke per pen lift and delays so each begins when the last ends; the signature pen shows the pacing. The look depends on the stroke widths and the order of paths, which a designer sets once. Rig questions: **unmeasured**. No study yet.

**Stepped typing.** Text is present in the page and its box is widened in character steps; a second animation blinks the caret. Deterministic and cheap, and the caret can be stopped at any frame. Rig questions: **unmeasured**. No study yet.

**Block, mask and blind wipes.** A solid block, a CSS mask, or a row of rotating slats crosses the frame to hide the old scene and reveal the new one. All are keyframes on transform, mask position or rotation, with nothing random, so they are candidates for scene transitions that can be seeked. Rig questions: **unmeasured**. No study yet.

**3D transforms for cubes, page flights and flips.** Faces or pages are placed with 3D transforms under a perspective parent, with preserve-3d on the container, and rotated by keyframes. The cube is a classic scene-to-scene device; the paper pirouette shows a single element travelling in depth. Rendering is deterministic, but small differences between browsers in 3D anti-aliasing are worth checking before relying on pixel-exact frames. Rig questions: **unmeasured**. No study yet.

**Card stacks, fans and swipes.** Cards share one keyframe set and differ by delay, rotation origin and z-order; the swipe pens move one card off and shift the rest. These map directly to our cards and rooms scenes and are plain CSS. Rig questions: **unmeasured**. No study yet.

**Icon micro-animations.** SVG parts of an icon are transformed individually so a control appears to react: dots become a close mark, a cart tips to receive an item, a button becomes a progress bar and then a tick. These are the building blocks for showing a control being used inside mocked chrome without any pointer at all. Rig questions: **unmeasured**. No study yet.

**Attention keyframes.** Shake, pulse, bounce and wobble sets on a single element, used to draw the eye to a field or button during a walkthrough. Trivially seekable; the only design question is restraint. Rig questions: **unmeasured**. No study yet.

## Not for us, and why

- **Illustrated scenes and characters** (Sausage Dog, Box Dog, Whale And The Moon, Moustached Nanny, Lighthouse, Coffee Machine, Submarine, Cassette, Amusement Park, Windmill, Candles, Solar Eclipse, Time Of Day, Saturn): charming and mostly deterministic, but they are decorative artwork with no place in a training video about a product. The techniques inside them are already covered by items above.
- **Evening Lanterns** (Braydon Coyer): described as generative, which means it is randomised at run time; no seed is shown, so two renders would differ.
- **Pure CSS Animated Bubbles, Animated SVG Snow, CSS Flame, Cloudy Spiral as decoration**: particle-style loops that may use random positions or long loops; fine as a loader idea, unsuitable as background unless every value is fixed.
- **Hover-only pens** (Books Hover, Tricky CSS Hover, Fancy Card Animation, CSS-only Border Animation, Button Shake): the motion is triggered by the pointer and stops without it. Only worth reading for the transition they perform; the rig would have to fire it on a timeline.
- **Product Page (Kaio Almeida), Card Swipe and CSS Animation Material Design as a look**: Material Design styling is Google's; we take only the timing idea, never a recreation of a third-party product's look.
- **Pens with embedded fonts or images from external hosts**: many older pens load Google Fonts or images from the pen's assets. Anything we rebuild has to open from a local file with no network calls, so assets must be replaced by local ones.
- **Motion blur pens** (Only CSS: Motion Blur, Animation With Offset Motion Blur): blur filters are expensive to render at full frame size and the effect reads poorly once compressed to video. Worth testing only if a study shows the cost is acceptable.
- **Licence caution on all of it**: CodePen's default MIT covers public pens, but a pen author can attach a different notice on the pen page, and Dev Snap itself says nothing. Read the pen page before copying, and put a row in LICENCES.md first.
