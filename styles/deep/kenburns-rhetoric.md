# Ken Burns and PBS rhetoric map (The American Revolution, episode 1, g5WkOr565I4)

From the broadcast captions: the first 25 minutes read closely, the rest counted. Raw.

## The three voices and how they are braided

The film has three speaking registers and a fourth, silence with music, and it rotates them on a cycle of roughly 30 to 90 s:

- **Narrator.** Past tense, declarative, long sentences built from lists and places, no first person, no address to the viewer, no hedging. Carries dates, numbers, names, movement ("In 1754, to solidify Britain's claim, the Royal Colony of Virginia dispatched militia..."). Reads with a fall at the end of every sentence.
- **Document voices** ("Voice:"), actors reading period texts, attributed at the end ("Thomas Paine," "Shingas, Lenape Nation," "[Washington]"). Present tense within the quote. The attribution-after rule holds every time: the words land, then the name. These are the film's evidence, in the sense that How Money Works uses a clipping; the frame under them is usually the artefact (a letter, a portrait) or a landscape.
- **Witnesses** (historians, "Man:", "Woman:", then named once the film has shown them: Vincent Brown, Christopher Brown, Taylor, Calloway, DuVal). Present tense, first person plural ("we tend to think"), hesitations kept ("uh," "I mean"), colloquial ("go to hell in a hand basket," "then you're really talking," "the British are really stupid"). They say what the narrator cannot: judgement, irony, the modern frame ("maps... are an argument rather than a conclusion").
- **Sound and silence.** Music cues (27 in the episode) and effects (93: gunfire, cannon, drums, a shouted "Fire!") punctuate, and the 68 s title hold at 3:24 is the film's longest silence of words.

A typical cycle from 11:12 to 14:39: narrator (the ambush, 70 s) with a shouted "Fire!" and gunfire inside it; narrator (the lesson, 40 s); witness (what Washington thought of London, 30 s); document voice (Washington's own words, 20 s), cannonfire; witness (the global war, 30 s). The witness and the document voice answer each other: the historian says "he thinks the British are really stupid," and then Washington's letter says it in his own register.

## Argument, in the first 25 minutes

1. **Epigraph and thesis (0:22 to 3:24).** A reading (Paine on the spark and the flame), an explosion, a drum; a Native reading (Canasatego on land); the narrator's first sentence sets a surprising frame: the Haudenosaunee union preceded the colonies' union; Franklin's "Join, or Die" is introduced through the Iroquois recommendation of union. The thesis of the series ("the most consequential revolution in history") arrives at 3:20, and the title.
2. **The thesis restated three ways (4:32 to 7:30).** Adams on the magnitude; the narrator's two-part sentence ("not just a clash... but a bloody struggle... that also somehow came to be about the noblest aspirations of humankind"); the catalogue of place names (the rivers, old names then new names), which is a device: a list read slowly over landscape is the film's way of saying "everywhere"; then "a savage civil war" and Abigail Adams on the cost. The thesis is given its contradiction at once: the noblest aspirations and tens of thousands killed.
3. **The witnesses define the stakes (7:33 to 9:34).** Creation myth; an origin story other countries lack and "a set of stakes... so far beyond what any set of events... can plausibly carry"; different places cohering "around a set of purposes and ideals." Then Washington's own line about "one patriotic band of brothers," which the film lets stand as the aspiration.
4. **The Native frame, first (9:54 to 11:12).** Shingas asks why the British come to fight on Native land; the narrator gives the demographic catastrophe in one sentence and the three claimants (Britain, France, "a host of Indian nations who had lived and farmed and hunted there for hundreds of generations"). The order matters: the Native claim is stated before the colonial story begins.
5. **Washington's first war as character study (11:12 to 14:39).** The ambush, the first shot "if so," the surrender "for the first and only time in his life," Braddock's defeat, "two horses were shot from under him," the two lessons stated outright ("British troops were not invincible, and there was no shame in retreating"), the rejected commission and the resentment. The historian's irony follows; then Washington's own letter.
6. **The war goes global, then the irony of victory (14:39 to 16:21).** "It mushrooms into a global campaign"; "just one piece on a broad, global imperial chessboard"; the victory, then the witness's reversal: "never wish for something too much... and then it all begins to go to hell in a hand basket." Music.
7. **Britishness, empire, slavery (16:21 to 21:24).** Boston's street names and shops; the new king; the mixed constitution praised; then Vincent Brown's correction of the frame (26 colonies, the Caribbean the most important, profit and slavery in the same sentence, "then you're really talking"); the population figure "Black and White"; Christopher Brown on markets and diversity; the Native majority on the continent and the refusal of the "timeless space" trope.
8. **Land as the engine (21:57 to 25:00).** Paine again on the improbability of union; the narrator's two-percent-own-two-thirds sentence on Britain versus "a land of opportunity" for free white men; Taylor on the resentment migrants brought; the three towns with over 10,000 people; "land and liberty are completely intertwined" and its corollary "means taking Native land. There is no other answer." The maps "are an argument rather than a conclusion." The Native nations named.

## Theses and subtext

- Stated, in the film's own order: union was a Native idea before it was a colonial one; the revolution was both the noblest of causes and a savage civil war; it started as a land war in the Ohio country and became a world war; Britain's victory created the conditions for its loss; liberty for white colonists was founded on land, and land meant Native land; slavery was everywhere and was where the money was.
- Subtext: the film makes the national myth and then complicates it in the same breath, and it does this by assigning the complication to a witness or a document voice rather than to the narrator. The narrator never editorialises; the historians do; the documents prove. The viewer is never told what to feel; the music and the silence tell them.
- Assumed viewer: an adult who knows the outline (Washington, Franklin, 1776) and will sit for two hours; proper nouns are given once with a gloss and then used; nothing is defined twice.
- Pace as argument: 99 to 123 wpm, with 34 silences over two seconds; the space between sentences is where the viewer does the work. The catalogue sentences (rivers, nations, places of origin in West Africa) are the slowest passages and the ones most likely to be remembered as "scope."

## Language mechanics

- Attribution after the quote, always.
- Numbers precise and few (22-year-old commander, July 9, 1755, 2 million, 2 percent own two-thirds, 26 colonies, 90 percent enslaved, 10,000).
- Triplets everywhere: "lived and farmed and hunted"; "laws, levied taxes, and decided how they'd be spent"; "politically, economically, and militarily."
- Hedges only where the record is uncertain ("if so, George Washington fired the very first shot"; "according to one of the Indians with the Virginians").
- The narrator's single rhetorical question in 25 minutes is in a quoted Native voice ("Why do you come to fight on our land?").
- Witnesses are allowed modern idiom and humour; the narrator is not. The two registers never leak into each other.

## Word and picture (from the frames viewed on the first pass; the shot log will add detail)

Artefact under a slow camera for the narrator and the document voices; present-day landscape at low light for the music and the catalogue sentences; the witness's face in a dark room for the witness; a hand-drawn period map with moving unit blocks for the one battle shown as a diagram. The camera move is timed to the sentence (the "Join, or Die" headline is revealed on the last word). There is no text on the frame other than titles and the interviewees' names; every number and every name is spoken.
