# IBM Technology rhetoric map ("Prompt to Production", bs1qPy_CWkM, 7:14)

From the manual-caption transcript (882 words) against the board timeline and the audio measurement. Raw.

## The shape

A thesis talk in four movements that match the four boards: plan, execute, verify, and the restatement. No hook beyond a contrast, no sponsor, no ident after the lower third, no call to action. 7 minutes, 125 wpm, six silences of 2 to 5 s while she writes.

1. **The contrast (0:00 to 0:33).** "Today, moving from prompt to production seems so simple with AI." The naive view is stated in the first person as a user would ("I just tell it my project idea, and agentic coding IDEs can get right to work"), then the pivot: "Let's explore what it actually takes and why writing code is increasingly becoming the easiest part of that journey from idea to outcome." The phrase "idea to outcome" is the frame; "prompt to production" is the slogan; both are written on the board by the end.
2. **Plan (0:33 to 2:25, board 1).** The analogy ("the same way you don't start pouring concrete before designing a building"), then a three-verb definition ("understand intent, assess impact, and reason about which steps are needed"), then a consequence stated as a law ("the quality of the solution is often determined long before that first line of code is even written"). Then a worked example in the first person ("Let's say I have a website") with four things the agent could build and the point that the prompter must "set the stage of what is important for success and why," with three constraints (data, budget, licensing). Then the generalisation: "much of modern software development is less about writing code and more about navigating dependencies, workflows, architecture decisions, and trade-offs." The section closes with a hinge sentence: "Once we've designed the right solution, it's time to have the AI execute, and that's where things get interesting."
3. **Execute (2:25 to 4:07, board 2).** The naive view again ("it's easy to see it as going from prompt to code... and are done"), then the list that becomes the board ("code, tests, configs, infrastructure, documentation, monitoring, pipelines, dependencies, security, compliance, the list goes on"), then the coordination claim and the definition of value ("isn't valuable due to its ability to write a simple function. It's valuable because of its ability to tie together execution, validation, dependencies, and testing all into one workflow"). The loop ("run tests, find any failures, fix those failures, run security scans, validate dependencies, and continually evaluate and rebuild") is the circle drawn on the board. The section ends on a question: "How do we know it worked?"
4. **Verify (4:07 to 6:16, board 3).** The history in one sentence ("for years, software development was limited by execution"), the change ("today, an agent can update hundreds of files... in a matter of minutes. That's output."), the thesis of the video: "AI can scale execution dramatically faster than humans can scale verification." The five-versus-500 files example in the first person. The five questions, which become the board's list. The line that carries the talk ("I don't care if AI changed 1000 lines of code, I care that the right 1000 lines changed"), the prescription ("it should generate evidence"), the definition of trust by negation ("Trust isn't generated by seeing more output or someone simply telling you the answer") and by affirmation (three clauses), and the slogan for the section: "confidence at scale."
5. **Restatement (6:16 to 7:10, board 4).** History again ("come a long way in just a few years"), the skill shift ("from planning to architecture to governance, engineering judgment and thinking have become even more valuable"), the two slogans in order ("We are now far past prompt to code. The future is prompt to production"), and the closing sentence that lists the four movements ("planning, execution, validation, and verification into a continuous path from idea to outcome").

## Theses and subtext

- Stated: planning determines quality; execution is coordination, not code; verification is the new bottleneck; evidence produces confidence; judgement matters more as implementation gets cheaper.
- Subtext: the talk is a position piece for a company that sells workflow tooling, but no product is named and no claim is made that cannot be argued from first principles; the authority is the structure, and the structure is on the board. The only first-person claims are modest ("I can review that myself," "I can't realistically review every change").
- Assumed viewer: a developer or an engineering manager who already uses coding agents; nothing is defined except by example; "agentic" is used without a gloss.
- Repetition as structure: the naive view is stated and overturned three times (prompt to code, prompt to code, output); the slogan is said at 0:00, 6:44 and 6:47; "idea to outcome" at 0:25 and 7:05; "execution" and "verification" appear in every movement.

## Language mechanics

- 64 sentences, average 13.8 words, one over 30. Short declaratives, many of them definitions ("That's output." "It should generate evidence.").
- Triplets: 11 ("understand intent, assess impact, and reason"; "execution, validation, dependencies, and testing"; "testing, validation, impact analysis, and explanations"; "planning to architecture to governance").
- Questions: 7, six of them in one run at 5:05 ("What changed? Why did it change? What could break? How does this impact downstream systems? And did we actually achieve the objective?") plus "How do we know it worked?" at 4:07. The audio measurement shows the questions are the loudest lines and the one-line answers ("That's output," "It should generate evidence," "The future is prompt to production") the quietest.
- Signposts: "Let's explore," "Let's say," "In fact," "As a consequence," "When used right," "Great AI workflows," "The future is."
- No humour, no hedges, no sources. The only citation is the audience's own experience.

## Word and picture

- Every list the voice says is written on the board as it is said, and every board resolves into one arrow chain, so the picture is the sentence's skeleton with the verbs removed. The relation is TXT throughout; the one picture (the wireframe) is ILL.
- The presenter's hands point at the item being discussed; the camera moves closer when she is making a claim without writing (4:10, 6:20) and wider when the board matters.
- Silence is where the writing is: the six pauses of 2 to 5 s fall on the longer words, and they are left in the edit. The viewer watches the word appear.
- The frame never jokes, never shows evidence from elsewhere, never shows a screen. The board is the only evidence, and it is evidence of the structure of the argument, not of the world.
