# Two channels: the voice argues, the picture editorialises

Group: word-and-picture. Say it like: "the picture makes a different joke", "say one thing, show another", "counterpoint", "the frame has its own opinion".

## Observed

- Fireship, The Code Report (I_KVMFrUtPk): 18 of 82 shots are a visual pun, a counterpoint or a reaction, and more than half of those are jokes the voice never makes (the orchestra puns on the sponsor's name, the gangster meme over the vendor, the whisper caption, the sofa caption, the twins' labels, the wojak). The voice stays on the argument throughout.
- Casually Explained: about a third of the visual jokes are the picture's own (the Michelangelo hand, the SEALs meme, the ethics-professor label, the crossed-out SIM photo, the host's face on a stranger); the rest amplify the voice's joke.

## The numbers

Fireship: one visual joke every 17 s; voice jokes about 4 a minute; the two rarely coincide. Casually: 25 of 78 frames carry a visual joke, about 8 of them independent of the voice.

## The effect

Two things happen at once and the viewer assembles them. The voice can stay fair and factual (the mechanism explained in two straight sentences) while the picture carries the attitude, so the argument is never interrupted by its own commentary, and a viewer who disagrees with the picture still gets the argument.

## Counter-example

IBM, AWS, Maarek and Ken Burns' narrator never do it: the picture is the sentence's skeleton (IBM), the sentence's nouns (AWS, Maarek) or the sentence's artefact (Ken Burns). In the documentary the attitude is assigned to a witness's voice instead of to the frame.

## What it needs

A second layer in the frame that is not the subject (a caption, a label, a reaction), timed independently of the voice; and a rule for when the second channel is allowed to speak.
