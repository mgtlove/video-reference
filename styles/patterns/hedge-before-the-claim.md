# The hedge before the biggest claim

Group: argument. Say it like: "from what I can tell", "say what you are not sure of", "admit the simplification", "trust devices".

## Observed

- Fireship (I_KVMFrUtPk): one hedge in five minutes ("from what I can tell"), placed before the biggest claim ("first time in history").
- Stephane Maarek (10JKpg-eqZU): the simplification admitted at the moment it is made ("obviously it's way more complicated but that's a simplified version"); the cost stated before the hands-on ("Route 53 is not free").
- How Money Works (W6xkZy9nkaI): 19 hedges ("reportedly," "for what it's worth," "to be fair," "I don't want to get your hopes up"), 4 admitted limits ("I don't want to go full armchair general... my insights... are effectively meaningless").
- AWS Developers (FAgmR9VV0GQ): stubs admitted ("it isn't actually reading the weather data"), "I myself am not a mathematician."
- Ken Burns and PBS (g5WkOr565I4): hedges only where the record is uncertain ("if so, George Washington fired the very first shot").

## The numbers

Hedges per video: Fireship 1, Casually 0, How Money Works 19, Maarek 1, AWS 0 (2 admitted limits), IBM 0, Ken Burns only on uncertain record. Named sources in the voice: How Money Works 2, Ken Burns after every reading, the rest 0.

## The effect

One hedge in the right place buys the whole video credibility; nineteen set a register (the sceptical reporter). Admitting a simplification or a stub at the moment it happens means the viewer never discovers it later and discounts everything.

## Counter-example

IBM makes no hedge and cites nothing; the argument is from first principles and the authority is the structure. Casually's confession at the open ("I dropped out") is the comedian's version: the credential is the admitted failure.

## What it needs

A narration rule: say the simplification, the cost or the stub in the sentence that makes it; a `verified: false` marker in the storyboard where the voice should hedge.
