# The naive view, then the overturn

Group: argument. Say it like: "start with what people think", "how hard can it be", "the obvious reading first", "set up the misconception".

## Observed

- IBM Technology (bs1qPy_CWkM): the naive view is stated in the first person as a user would ("I just tell it my project idea...") and overturned three times across the talk (prompt to code; prompt to code; "That's output.").
- AWS Developers (FAgmR9VV0GQ): "Call a model, get a response. How hard can it be?" then six questions a beginner fears, then "you really don't."
- How Money Works (W6xkZy9nkaI): the sceptical reading is always stated before the argument against it ("there is the reasonable argument that this might not be much more than a run-of-the-mill trade deal"), and the recurring move is to call a thing meaningless and then show it matters.

## The numbers

IBM: 3 overturns in 7 minutes, each at a movement boundary. How Money Works: the "nothing burger" granted at 9:04 and refuted by one observation. AWS: 6 questions in the first 50 s, almost none after.

## The effect

Stating the viewer's own first thought makes the correction land as a discovery rather than a lecture, and gives each section a turn. Granting the sceptical reading first is also a trust device: the narrator has considered it.

## Counter-example

Maarek never states a misconception; he defines and moves on, which is faster and fits a reference lecture. Fireship states the establishment's answer fairly and then undercuts it, a cousin of this move with a joke at the turn.

## What it needs

A storyboard beat per section that states the naive view in the viewer's words before the explanation; nothing in the rig.
