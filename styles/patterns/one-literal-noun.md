# One literal noun per sentence

Group: word-and-picture. Say it like: "show the word", "a picture for every sentence", "b-roll that matches the noun", "wallpaper footage".

## Observed

- How Money Works (W6xkZy9nkaI): every sentence has a picture under it that is literal to one noun in it. "Trade" is a container ship three times, "finance" is the stock exchange facade, "Canada" a skyline with a flag, "the UK" Westminster, "negotiate" hands at a table, "obligations" soldiers, "the EU" flags outside a glass building. The vocabulary is small and repeats.
- AWS Developers (FAgmR9VV0GQ): in a recording the picture is literal at the clause level ("I'll select upload" and the cursor clicks upload).

## The numbers

195 shots in 17 minutes; 37 percent evidence, 36 percent illustration from stock; the same dozen nouns pictured again and again; mean shot 5.2 s; camera drift on 149 of 195.

## The effect

Because the vocabulary repeats until it is invisible, the viewer stops reading the pictures and listens; the frame confirms that the voice is still on topic and asks nothing of the eye. It is a way of filling seventeen minutes without a single frame that needs attention.

## Counter-example

Fireship's evidence frames demand reading (a highlighted line in a document); Maarek's slide is the whole lesson. A literal-noun frame under a sentence that needs the picture read (a number, a mechanism) hides the point.

## What it needs

A library of a few dozen stills or loops, each tagged with the noun it stands for; a drift on every still so a hold never reads as a slide (see `drift-on-every-still`).
