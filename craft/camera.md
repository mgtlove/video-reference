## What a video maker needs to know

**Shot sizes.** Film names framings by how much of a person they show: long shot shows "the spatial relations among the important figures, objects, and setting" (Yale), medium shot about three quarters of the body, close-up the head, extreme close-up a feature (UVM). On a software screen the same ladder is: the whole screen (long shot: where things are), a region such as a panel or form (medium: what we are working in), one control (close-up: what to click), and a value or icon (extreme close-up: what to read). The long shot establishes; without it, the close-up has no address. Every walkthrough starts wide at least once so the viewer can find the region later in the real product.

**Push-in versus cut.** A push-in moves the camera toward the subject and reads as growing importance or a realisation; a cut to a close-up is a static framing choice (StudioBinder: "a camera push-in shot can reveal the character's state of mind"). On a screen, a push-in says "this, out of all of that", and it keeps the viewer's map intact because they watched the trip. A cut says "now look here" and costs them a moment to re-find where they are. Push in when the next subject is inside the current frame; cut when it is somewhere else, or when the change is a change of subject rather than a narrowing.

**The Ken Burns effect.** A slow pan or zoom over a still image (Wikipedia). It works because a still that moves is read as time passing and as a look, and the move can lead the eye across the picture. Burns himself warns against "mindless, anxious movement": the meaning is in the duration, not the motion. Glaser and colleagues measured it: a minimal zoom-in increased gaze coherence and dwell time on the centre, while a zoom-out behaved like a static image, so zoom-ins direct attention and zoom-outs do not. For speed, no standards body publishes a number. Nedomansky's push-in presets change scale by 5, 10, 15, 20 or 25% over the clip, and he recommends 5 or 10% as the ones "almost indistinguishable from a practical dolly push-in". More than that over one shot is a move the viewer notices; that is fine when the move is the point, and wrong when the still is.

**Motivated moves.** "Every time you move your camera, you're speaking to your audience" (Renée). A move is motivated when something on screen or in the story asks for it: a subject moves, a hand gestures, a detail is revealed (McCullagh). In a narrated explainer the narration is the subject. The camera moves because the voice moved attention, and it moves after the voice, not before, so the viewer hears the reason and then sees the answer. An unmotivated move is noise; the formal viewer calls it fidgeting.

**Ease.** A camera that starts and stops at constant speed looks mechanical; ease-in and ease-out make it "slow down before it stops" (Toon Boom). Every move eases.

**Holds.** No film source gives a hold time; it is judgement bound by reading. A subject must be still long enough to be read: Netflix's floor for a subtitle event is five-sixths of a second, and a control with a label is at least that much reading. Hold before a cut so the last frame settles, hold after a move so the new frame is read before anything else happens.

**Cut, fade, wipe.** The cut is the invisible transition; a dissolve says time or place changed within continuing action; a fade marks a major boundary, an opening or a close; a wipe does a dissolve's job while calling attention to itself (Videomaker). Yale: a wipe signals "a brief temporal ellipsis and a direct connection between the two images". In feature films "almost 99% of all transitions between shots in popular film are now cuts" (Cutting et al.). Cut within a topic; fade between parts; use a wipe only when the connection between two screens is the lesson, for example before and after a setting change.

**Screen direction.** "Movement from one edited shot to another must maintain the consistency of screen direction" (Wikipedia); consistent direction "helps establish the geography of the scene" (Nashville State). A pointer that travels left to right in one shot and right to left in the next has, to the viewer, turned around. Match the direction of pans, pointer travel and card entrances across a cut, or put a still frame between them.

**Pace.** Feature films have gone from about 10 seconds per shot in the 1930s to under 4 seconds after 2000 (Cutting et al.), and action films sit near 4 seconds while horror sits near 16 (Follows). Those are entertainment numbers made to hold attention, not to teach. For teaching, Guo, Kim and Rubin found engagement fell after 6 minutes and rose with instructors who spoke faster and with personal, close framing. Nobody has published a tolerated number of camera moves per minute for instructional video. The safe reading of the evidence is: motion holds attention but spends it, and a learner also needs to read, so instructional footage moves less often than a film cuts.

**Resolution.** Digital zoom is cropping and scaling up: "no optical resolution is gained" (Wikipedia). A screenshot enlarged past its own pixels goes soft, and text goes soft first. Dynatrace asks for captures at 1920x1080 at least and 3840x2160 for events, which is the same rule from the other side: capture at the largest size the camera will ever show.

## For our stage

The camera in the rig travels over a stage larger than the 1920x1080 frame (`camTravel`), so a push-in is a change of camera scale and a pan is a change of camera position; both are deterministic and seekable, which is why a still-frame check can measure them. The shot ladder maps to camera scale on a recreated screen: whole screen fits the picture area (1440x880), a region fills it, a control fills about a third of it. Because the picture area is 1440 wide and a screenshot of a 1920-wide product is 1920 wide, "whole screen" is already a slight zoom out (scale 0.75) and a "control" close-up at scale 2.0 shows 720 source pixels across the picture area, which is where the resolution rule starts to bite: a 1x screenshot allows no more than scale 1.0 before it softens; a 2x capture allows scale 2.0.

The rail and the caption band do not move with the camera. The key phrase in the rail changes on the voice, and the camera lands after the phrase changes, so the sequence a viewer experiences is: hear it, see the phrase, see the camera arrive. Captions must be readable during a move, which is one reason the band is fixed and moves are slow.

What the rig already does right: moves are eased; the camera lands on a target rather than a coordinate, so a landing on a third (see `composition.md`) is a matter of picking the target; pointer travel (`cursorTo`) is a path, so its direction can be kept consistent across cuts. What it does not do: it does not refuse a scale that exceeds the screenshot's pixels, it does not enforce a hold after a move, and it does not count moves per minute. Those are timeline checks and reviewer judgement.

## Rules

| Id | Rule | Why | Checkable |
|---|---|---|---|
| C-CAM-1 | Every walkthrough opens with a whole-screen shot (the screen fitting the picture area) before any region or control shot. | The long shot establishes where things are (Yale, UVM). | yes: first shot on a recreated screen has the whole screen inside the picture area |
| C-CAM-2 | Move to a subject inside the current frame with a push-in; go to a subject outside it with a cut, or with a pan when the trip itself is the lesson. | Push-in keeps the viewer's map; a cut is for a change of subject (StudioBinder, Videomaker). | judgement, with a measurable part: a push-in's target lies inside the frame before the move |
| C-CAM-3 | A camera move starts only after the voice has named its subject, within a short lag, never before the voice. | Motivated movement: the voice moves attention, the camera follows (Renée, McCullagh). | yes: move start time >= the word's onset in the transcript timing; house default lag 0.2 to 0.6 s, not a sourced number |
| C-CAM-4 | Every move eases in and out; no move starts or stops at full speed. | Constant-speed camera looks mechanical (Toon Boom). | yes: camera position or scale curve has zero velocity at both ends |
| C-CAM-5 | A Ken Burns move on a still changes scale by 5 to 10% over the shot in normal tone; more than 25% is not a Ken Burns move but a push-in and is treated as one. | Nedomansky's 5 and 10% presets read as natural; 25% is his largest. Speed beyond that is judgement. | yes: scale at end divided by scale at start between 1.05 and 1.10 for a still; a still with scale change above 1.25 fails the rule |
| C-CAM-6 | After a move ends, the frame holds still for at least five-sixths of a second plus reading time for any text now in view, before the next move or cut. | Netflix's floor for a readable event; reading time is the floor for the new frame (judgement beyond that). | yes: gap between move end and next move or cut >= 0.83 s; longer when a label is in view (judgement) |
| C-CAM-7 | Before a cut, the outgoing frame is still for a settle interval. | The last frame must settle so the cut is invisible (Videomaker). | yes: no camera motion in the final 0.5 s before a cut; the 0.5 s is a house choice |
| C-CAM-8 | Cut within a topic; fade only between parts; wipe only when the relation between the two screens is the lesson. | Transition grammar (Videomaker, Yale); cuts are 99% of film transitions (Cutting et al.). | yes: transition type against the part boundary in the timeline; the "lesson" test is judgement |
| C-CAM-9 | A fade or dissolve lasts long enough to be seen as one; a shorter one is a cut and should be written as a cut. | Material's interface floor of 150 to 200 ms for a transition to be understood; the video number is judgement above that. | yes: dissolve duration >= 0.2 s; house default 0.5 s |
| C-CAM-10 | Camera pans, pointer travel and card entrances keep the same screen direction across a cut, or a still frame sits between them. | Screen direction continuity (Wikipedia, Nashville State). | yes: sign of x and y travel on either side of a cut match, or the incoming shot begins with a still |
| C-CAM-11 | The camera never shows a screenshot at more than its native pixel size: rendered pixels per source pixel <= 1.0 at every frame. | Digital zoom gains no resolution (Wikipedia); capture at the largest size shown (Dynatrace). | yes: camera scale times screenshot placement scale <= 1.0 against the source bitmap at every frame of the timeline |
| C-CAM-12 | A close-up is achieved by capturing at 2x and scaling down, never by scaling a 1x capture up. | Same as C-CAM-11, stated for the brief stage. | yes: source bitmap width >= picture area width times the maximum camera scale used on it |
| C-CAM-13 | At most one camera move per narrated sentence, and never two moves without a hold that meets C-CAM-6 between them. | No published tolerated rate exists for instructional video; this is judgement informed by Guo et al. (attention is finite) and Burns (meaning accrues in duration). | yes as a count: moves per sentence <= 1 on the timeline; the rate itself is a house choice |
| C-CAM-14 | Whole-video pace: fewer camera moves per minute than a feature film has cuts, as a sanity ceiling, and far fewer in formal tone. | Cutting et al.: under 4 s per shot after 2000 is entertainment pacing; teaching needs reading time. | yes: moves per minute <= 15 in normal tone (a ceiling derived from the 4 s figure, a house reading, not a sourced instructional number); formal tone lower, see below |
| C-CAM-15 | Zoom-outs are not used to direct attention; they are used only to re-establish or to end a part. | Zoom-outs measured like a static image for attention (Glaser et al.). | judgement, with a measurable part: any scale decrease is followed by a whole-screen or part-boundary shot |
| C-CAM-16 | Motion across the frame is small and slow enough to be watched by someone sensitive to motion; no full-frame parallax, no move faster than the ease allows. | WCAG 2.3.3 on motion that can trigger vestibular symptoms; essential motion is allowed, gratuitous motion is not. | judgement, with a measurable part: no move covers more than the picture area's width in under 1 s; the 1 s is a house choice |

## Turned down

Formal tone keeps C-CAM-1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 10, 11, 12 and 15 unchanged: establishing shots, motivation, easing, holds, direction, and resolution are not style. What it changes: Ken Burns moves on stills drop to the 5% end of C-CAM-5 or to none; the moves-per-sentence rule tightens to one move per two sentences and the pace ceiling in C-CAM-14 halves; push-ins replace cuts wherever the subject is already in frame, because a push-in is quieter than a cut; fades between parts get a little longer, not shorter, so the boundary is calm. It must not change: the hold after a move (a formal viewer reads more, not less), the resolution rule, and the rule that the camera follows the voice. A formal video that has turned motion down still moves; it just never moves without being asked.
