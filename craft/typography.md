## What a video maker needs to know

**Size is set by the smallest screen, not the render.** The frame is 1920x1080, but it is watched on a laptop at roughly half scale and on a phone at a fifth. Broadcasters size subtitles as a fraction of picture height, which scales cleanly: the BBC guideline, as summarised by Broadcast Writer, sets subtitle line height at 8% of the active video height for 16:9, which is 86px of line height at 1080, so a font size of about 69 to 72px at a 1.2 to 1.25 line-height ratio (our arithmetic). IMSC 1.3, the subtitle standard under most streaming captions, uses a default grid of 32 by 15 cells, so 100% font size is one cell, "6.66% (100/15)" of the container: 72px at 1080. Those two agree within a few pixels and are the number to trust for captions. WCAG's "large text" of 18pt, about 24px, is a screen-at-arm's-length figure; scaled to a phone held in portrait, where the frame is around 390 CSS px wide (our assumption, not a sourced figure), 24px on the phone is about 118px on the frame, and in landscape at about 850 CSS px wide it is about 54px. So: captions and rail text no smaller than 72px on the frame; body text on cards no smaller than 54px, and any text under that is decoration the narration does not depend on. The 54px floor is our derivation, and is judgement.

**Line length.** Bringhurst, quoted by Rutter: 45 to 75 characters is satisfactory and 66 is ideal; Butterick allows 45 to 90. That is for reading a page. A video line is read while listening, so shorter: Netflix caps subtitle lines at 42 characters, and the BBC summary gives 37 for broadcast teletext and 68% of the width for online. Take 42 as the cap for any line of text on our frame and 30 to 36 as the comfortable range.

**Line height.** Butterick: 120% to 145% of the point size. WCAG SC 1.4.12 asks that text still work at 1.5 line height, 0.12em letter spacing and 0.16em word spacing, which is a robustness test, not a target. Broadcaster captions run tighter (the 8% BBC figure is line height, and it holds two lines in 16% of the frame). Use 1.25 for captions and rail, 1.35 to 1.45 for card body.

**Weight on a dark ground.** Light text on dark looks heavier than the same weight on white; Argyle: "white text on a black background looks thicker". Two responses: use a regular rather than a medium for body, or, with a variable font, lower the GRAD (grade) axis, which changes darkness without changing width. Do not go thin: Android TV's guide warns that "thinner lines are not instantly recognisable", and compression eats thin strokes. NN/g (Budiu, 2020) finds light mode reads better for most people, so a dark ground is a deliberate trade for calm and for product screens; it means we owe the reader more size and more contrast, not less.

**Tabular numerals.** A counter that ticks (steps, percentages, timers) must not shift sideways as digits change. `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` (OpenType `tnum`) selects figures "where numbers are all of the same size" (MDN). Not every face has them; the theme's face must, or counters use a face that does.

**How many words, for how long.** Subtitle research gives the ceiling for reading while watching. Szarkowska and Gerber-Morón tested 12, 16 and 20 characters per second and found viewers coped with 20; the traditional six-second rule (a full two-line subtitle held six seconds) is about 12 cps or 140 to 150 words per minute, and English SDH commonly runs up to 180 wpm. The BBC summary gives 160 to 180 wpm with a minimum of about 0.3 seconds per word. DCMP, for educational video, caps at 130, 140 and 160 wpm by school level. Netflix: up to 20 cps for adults, 17 for children, each event at least 5/6 of a second and at most 7 seconds. Arithmetic at 160 wpm: 2.7 words a second, so a phrase of 8 words needs 3 seconds on screen before it is read, and a frame that changes text faster than that is not being read. On-screen text competes with the narration for the same reading, so it should carry fewer words than the narration does over the same seconds: a rail phrase of 3 to 7 words, a card of at most two short lines. Those counts are judgement built on the published rates.

**Caption conventions.** White text (BBC summary; DCMP prefers white, medium weight, sans serif, with a shadow or a translucent box) on a black or near-black band, centred at the bottom (Netflix), at most two lines (Netflix, BBC summary, DCMP), inside the title-safe area. SMPTE ST 2046-1, via NAB, puts safe title at 90% of width and height and safe action at 93%; the older 80% and 90% figures are CRT-era. At 1080 that is a 96px margin left and right and 54px top and bottom. Mixed case, not capitals (DCMP).

**All caps and letter-spacing.** Butterick: all caps is "Fine for less than one line", never for paragraphs, and caps need 5% to 12% extra letter-spacing; lowercase text is not letter-spaced. On video this is stricter, because caps have no ascender and descender shapes to help a reader at small scale. A caps label is a category tag, not a sentence.

**Font licensing.** A rendered video contains pixels, not the font file. Adobe Fonts permits film and video use and says recipients need no licence for "rasterized or properly embedded font data". The SIL OFL welcomes any design work and allows bundling the font with software as long as it is not sold on its own, and embedding in documents. Commercial foundries often sell a separate video licence; TypeType's says the licensee "shall not distribute or make publicly available the Font files". The line that matters for us: a `.mp4` is always fine under any of these; a rig package that ships `.woff2` files in `fonts/` is redistribution, and only OFL (or an explicit web or app licence) allows it. Segoe UI is a Windows system font and cannot be shipped in the package at all; the rig may name it in a stack but must ship an OFL face as the one it renders with, so renders match on every machine.

## For our stage

The rig renders headless at 1920x1080, so nothing scales the text but the viewer's player. That makes size the first check. The caption band is the one place the numbers are firm: 72px, line height 1.25, two lines, chalk on near-black at 90% width, bottom edge at or above 54px from the frame's bottom. The right rail's key phrase is read once while the narration says it, so it wants 72px or more, 3 to 7 words, wrapping at 30 to 36 characters, never a paragraph. Card body text lives at 54px or more; a card that needs a smaller size to fit has too many words for a frame, and the brief should split it. Counters use tabular figures.

The theme's `--face` token in the studies is a system stack ("Segoe UI", Arial, Helvetica). That renders differently per machine, is not shippable, and Segoe's regular is heavy on dark; the kit should ship one OFL face with tabular figures and a grade or a lighter regular, and the studies should follow. Weight for body on the stage is regular, with medium reserved for the rail phrase and the one emphasised word in a card.

Recreated product screens keep the product's type at the product's size, which on a full-screen recreation is far below 54px. That is allowed because the screen is recognised, not read; the words a learner must read are repeated in the rail or the caption band, and the camera moves in when a field's value matters. The walkthrough's camera is the size control for screens, not the CSS.

Every caption event has a duration, so the check can measure reading load directly: characters shown divided by seconds shown, against 20 cps, and words against 160 wpm.

## Turned down

The formal tone changes weight and rhythm, not size. It may drop the rail phrase from medium to regular, remove letter-spaced caps labels in favour of mixed case, hold each phrase a little longer (still within the 7 s caption maximum), and use one face throughout. It must not change: minimum sizes (C-TYPE-1 to C-TYPE-3), line length (C-TYPE-4), reading rate limits (C-TYPE-8, C-TYPE-9), safe title (C-TYPE-12) or contrast (C-TYPE-14). A formal video that shrinks its type to look restrained is quieter for the maker and harder for the learner; restraint comes from fewer words per frame, not smaller ones.
